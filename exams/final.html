The synthesis exam combines standards you have already been assessed on, with partial credit, and it does not tell you which relations apply: choosing them is part of the task. It contains nothing new. Your score moves your standards-based grade up one level (85% and above), keeps it (70–85%) or moves it down one level (below 70%).

Part 1How to attack a synthesis problem

  1. Draw the device and number the stations. Use the course stations (0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 9, 13, 19, or chamber/throat/exit).
  2. Name the process between each pair of stations. Area change only → isentropic (C1). Shock → C2. Friction in a constant-area duct → Fanno (C3). Heat or combustion in a constant-area duct → Rayleigh (C4) plus an energy balance (C6). Work → compressor/turbine with efficiencies (C8). Wall heat → resistances and correlations (C12/C13).
  3. List what is conserved across each process (\(T_t\)? \(p_t\)? \(\dot m\)? \(A^*\)?). That tells you which ratio to carry forward.
  4. Carry stagnation properties through the chain and only convert to static where you need velocity, pressure thrust or heat flux.
  5. Sanity-check every station with the J1 trends. Partial credit rewards a clear chain even when a number slips.

Part 4E1 · The ethics standard

E1: I can identify my core values and use them to reason through an ethical dilemma in aerospace engineering. It is a pass-gate: you need it for any passing grade. The paper is assigned in week 10 alongside the history of US rockets lecture. A structure that works:

  1. Name your core values (three or four), and say where each comes from and what it demands of you in practice. "Honesty" is a word; "I report test data that hurts my schedule" is a value in action.
  2. State the dilemma precisely: who decides, what the options are, who bears the risk, and what is uncertain.
  3. Identify the stakeholders and the obligations you have to each: the public (paramount, in the NSPE and AIAA codes), your employer, your colleagues, yourself.
  4. Reason from your values to a decision, showing where two values conflict and how you resolve it.
  5. Test the decision: would you defend it publicly? What would you do if your manager disagreed? What is your escalation path?

Well-documented aerospace cases to think with: the Challenger O-ring launch decision (cold-temperature data, the "take off your engineering hat" moment), Columbia foam strike assessment, the Apollo 1 fire, the DC-10 cargo door, and the 737 MAX MCAS certification. Several connect directly to this course: solid-motor joints, combustor and nozzle thermal limits, and test-versus-schedule pressure.
